Overview

A guide to sorting that stays motivating

The challenge

Young people in cities often struggle to recycle correctly. They don’t know what goes where, sorting feels like effort without a payoff, and nothing shows them what difference it makes.

  • Low engagement with recycling among young urban residents
  • Confusion about what can and can’t be recycled
  • No immediate feedback on environmental impact

The solution

Recycle Ready makes sorting easy to learn and rewarding to keep up, in four parts.

  • Scan & search. Scan a barcode or search to see exactly how to sort a product.
  • Map. Find nearby stations, filter by material, get directions.
  • Facts. Short answers and an A to Z of recycling topics.
  • Points & rewards. Level up, compare with friends, unlock rewards.

The redesigned app

The redesigned app

In 2026 I rebuilt the interface from scratch: a new colour system and type scale, reusable components, larger and darker text, and an English UI. The structure stayed the same, because testing had already settled it.

01 · Research

Watching people sort

We started with contextual observation and interviews with three people: one environmentally conscious adult and two young people. We watched them sort their own waste and asked why they do it the way they do.

What we asked

Why do you do it that way?
How do you think when you recycle something?
Do you usually recycle?
When don’t you sort, and why?
Is the problem the packaging, not knowing, or being unsure?

Sessions were recorded, and we took notes while watching each person sort at home.

What we found

Knowing isn’t the problem. Doing it is.

The most environmentally aware participant explained the impact of recycling at length. In practice she still slipped, sometimes from laziness, sometimes because the item itself was confusing.

Effort decides.

One person won’t walk back to a bin she has already passed, so she uses the nearest one. With items made of three or more materials, people bin them together under the biggest material.

Some things have no obvious home.

Kitchen paper, a dishcloth, a tea bag, a pen, a lamp, old electronics. Tearing parts apart with scissors was where people gave up.

Not knowing leads to doubt.

Packaging doesn’t always say where it goes. That gap in knowledge turns into uncertainty, even about small things.

What we took forward

An app that can identify an item and say which bin it belongs in, with a simple search to find it. Maybe even recognise several items at once and colour-code them by type. This is where scan and search began.

So we asked

How might we make recycling easy to learn, quick to do and worth keeping up?

Reflection

What I took away

1

Users set the priorities

Participants asked for clear sorting information, a map and visible impact. Those three things shaped the whole app.

2

Two prototypes taught us more than one

One was calm and one was useful. The real design work was merging them, backed by what testers said.

3

Icons need words

One unlabelled button was enough to stall a tester, so the tab bar and key actions in the redesign carry labels.

If I kept going

  • Test the redesign with people outside our own circle.
  • Measure task success and time on task, and add a SUS score.
  • Build the category and multi-part product flows from the seminar feedback.
  • Bring back CO₂ per scan and neighbourhood totals from the participants’ sketches.
